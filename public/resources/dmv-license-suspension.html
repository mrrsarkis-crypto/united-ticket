    DMV License Suspension in California

    Last updated September 2026 Educational resource Written by the United Traffic Tickets Defense editorial team

    In California, the courts and the DMV run separate-but-linked systems. A traffic ticket is handled by the court, but the DMV can independently hold or suspend your license for things the court reports — most commonly failure to appear and failure to pay. Understanding which agency did what is the first step to getting back on the road.

    Not legal advice. This is general education. Your local county and court set the exact forms and procedures. If you have an active warrant, a serious charge, or a CDL at risk, talk to a qualified attorney promptly.

    Hold vs. suspension vs. revocation

    • Hold: A block (often due to an unpaid ticket or unresolved case) that prevents renewal. Not technically a suspension, but it stops you from getting a new license or registration.
    • Suspension: Your driving privilege is temporarily withdrawn after a hearing or notification. This includes negligent-operator suspension for repeat violations.
    • Revocation: Your license is canceled; you must typically reapply (more common for serious matters like DUI).

    The court vs. the DMV

    The court fines you for the violation. But under Vehicle Code § 40509, the court tells the DMV when you fail to appear or fail to pay, and the DMV then holds your license. That means even if you later pay the fine, you may still need to get the hold lifted at the DMV separately.

    Negligent-operator (PRIOR) suspension

    Under Vehicle Code § 12810.5, too many points in a rolling period can make you a negligent operator and lead to suspension or probation. Points stay on your record for 36 months for most violations (longer for serious ones). See how a ticket affects your record.

    How to reinstate

    1. Identify which hold or suspension you have. You can check online at the DMV or by contacting their driver safety office.
    2. For a court-reported hold: resolve the ticket with the court and pay any DUI/assessment, then ask the court to clear the report to the DMV.
    3. For a negligent-operator suspension: the DMV usually requires you to maintain a clean record for a period, and pay a reinstatement fee.
    4. Ship your proof of insurance (SR-22 only when the DMV specifically asks for it) if required.
    Don't drive on a suspended license. Driving while suspended can carry serious consequences on its own. Sort out the hold or suspension before you get behind the wheel.
