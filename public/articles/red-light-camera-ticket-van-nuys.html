Getting a red light camera ticket in Van Nuys feels different from getting pulled over. There is no officer, no roadside conversation — just a letter with a grainy photo of your car and a fine that can run several hundred dollars. But in Los Angeles County, that letter may not be what it appears to be, and you may have more options than the notice suggests.

The first question: citation or "notice of violation"?

Not every mailed camera ticket is an enforceable citation. Many mailings are titled something like a "notice of violation" — a vendor-generated letter that tells you to pay or log into a website. A true citation is a court document: issued by an officer or properly filed and served, carrying a court date and case number. Only those generally carry consequences like license holds, collections, or a failure-to-appear charge.

A notice of violation that was never converted into a filed citation often has no enforceable judgment behind it. That distinction matters enormously — people pay mailed notices assuming they are court orders when, in many cases, they are not. If you received a red light camera ticket in Los Angeles and are unsure which one you have, do not treat the envelope as final. Get the document reviewed before you pay anything or log into any payment portal.

Why so many LA County camera tickets go nowhere

Here is the documented history, stated carefully: LA County's camera programs were largely discontinued in the early 2010s. Los Angeles itself and many surrounding cities ended their red light camera contracts, and enforcement by mail has wound down dramatically since. That is why drivers in Van Nuys, Burbank, and elsewhere sometimes receive notices that reference programs that are barely active — or letters from collection vendors long after the cameras stopped generating new citations.

If you searched for "red light camera ticket Burbank" or "red light camera ticket Los Angeles" and landed here, the same principles apply countywide. The age of the program, the status of the vendor, and whether the notice ever became a filed case all affect what — if anything — you actually owe. An old notice is not automatically dead, but it is also not automatically enforceable. The details decide.

Defenses that work against camera evidence

When a camera citation is a real, filed case, it still has to be proven. Camera tickets are unusually vulnerable to procedural defenses, because the entire case rests on photographs and records maintained by a private vendor — not on a witness in uniform.

Three defenses that show up again and again

  • Identity of the driver. A photo of a license plate is not a photo of a person. If the images do not clearly identify who was driving, the prosecution still has to prove you were behind the wheel.
  • Calibration and maintenance. Cameras must be calibrated and maintained on schedule, and vendors must document it. Gaps in maintenance records can undermine the reliability of the timing and the images.
  • Chain of custody. The photos have to be authenticated — collected properly, transmitted securely, stored without alteration. Breaks in that chain give a defense attorney real arguments in a written declaration.

These defenses are raised through a trial by written declaration (form TR-205): you submit your defense in writing to the court instead of appearing in person. In many cases this is the most practical way to fight a camera ticket, because the whole case is documentary anyway. You prepare the declaration, the court reviews the written evidence on both sides, and it may be dismissed or reduced — often without you ever stepping into a courtroom.
