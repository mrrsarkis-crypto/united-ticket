HOV Lane Ticket in California (VC 21655.5): Fines and Real Defenses

One solo trip in the carpool lane can cost you close to $500 — plus a point on your record and years of higher insurance. Here is what Vehicle Code 21655.5 actually requires, who counts as an occupant, and the defenses that hold up.

What VC 21655.5 Actually Says

California Vehicle Code 21655.5 makes it an infraction to drive in a lane designated for high-occupancy vehicles unless your vehicle meets the posted occupancy requirement — typically 2 or more, or 3 or more persons, depending on the signs. The key word is posted: the restriction only applies where the lane is properly designated by official signs and markings. HOV lanes cover most LA freeways — the 405, 101, 10, 110 — and enforcement is heaviest during posted carpool hours. Outside those hours, many HOV lanes revert to general-purpose lanes, which is exactly where a lot of bad tickets get written.

The Real Cost: Fine, Point, and Insurance

The base fine for a VC 21655.5 violation looks modest on paper, but California’s penalty assessments roughly quadruple the total — drivers typically pay around $490 or more for a first offense.

Then comes the part the citation doesn’t spell out: the conviction is generally a one-point moving violation (3 years and 3 months on your record), insurers commonly surcharge it for years, and points accumulate toward negligent-operator thresholds — 4 points in 12 months can mean a suspended license.

A “quick” carpool-lane shortcut can easily become a $1,500 problem spread over three years — worth a free look before you pay, since paying turns the ticket into a conviction.

Who Counts as an Occupant?

This is the most misunderstood part of the law, and it cuts both ways. California counts any person as an occupant for HOV purposes, regardless of age — a baby in a car seat is an occupant. If you were cited with two people in the car because the officer “couldn’t see” a small child, that is a defense, not a technicality. Motorcycles may use HOV lanes at any time, even with only the rider, unless a specific sign says otherwise — a surprising number of motorcycle HOV tickets get written and dismissed. Buses, emergency vehicles, and other vehicles the signs specifically allow are also exempt. What does not count: pets, and the old mannequin-in-the-passenger-seat trick. It has to be a person.

Defenses That Actually Hold Up

HOV tickets are unusually beatable as traffic citations go, because the violation turns on what the officer could see — and officers are often wrong about what they saw. None of these are guarantees; every defense depends on the actual facts of your stop. The ones that work:

1. The officer couldn’t see your passengers

The most common winning defense. Tinted rear windows, a sleeping child lying down, a passenger reclined in the back seat — if the officer genuinely could not see a qualifying occupant, the observation the ticket rests on is unreliable. Photos of your windows and seating arrangement help enormously.

2. Signage problems

The lane must be properly designated. Missing, obscured, or contradictory signs — or HOV hours that had already ended when you entered the lane — undermine the charge. If the signs said the restriction ended at 7 PM and you entered at 7:05, document it.

3. Motorcycle or exempt vehicle

If you were on a motorcycle or in another exempt vehicle class, the ticket should never have been written.

Many HOV tickets are eligible for trial by written declaration — fought by mail on form TR-205, no courtroom visit. A written declaration is a natural fit for HOV cases because the defense is usually photographic and factual: window tint, signage photos, timestamps.

Mistakes That Sink HOV Fights

Arguing “everyone does it.” Selective enforcement is not a defense to the facts of your stop. Stick to what happened in your car, on that road, at that time.
Waiting until the deadline to gather evidence. Signage gets fixed, memories fade, dashcam footage loops over itself. Collect photos and video in the first days after the ticket.
Paying before checking. Payment is generally recorded as a conviction — fine, point, and all. A free ticket scan takes a minute; the conviction lasts years.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is an HOV lane ticket in California?

The base fine for a VC 21655.5 violation is relatively low, but state and county penalty assessments typically push the total to around $490 or more. It is also a one-point moving violation on your driving record.

Does a baby count as a passenger in the carpool lane in California?

California counts any person as an occupant for HOV purposes, including infants and children in car seats. The law looks at the number of people in the vehicle, not their age.

Can motorcycles use the HOV lane in California?

Yes. Motorcycles may use HOV lanes in California at any time, even with only the rider, unless a specific sign says otherwise.

Is an HOV lane ticket a moving violation in California?

Yes. A VC 21655.5 conviction is generally a one-point moving violation, which stays on your record for 3 years and 3 months and can raise your insurance premiums.

Can you fight an HOV lane ticket by written declaration?
